Every week somebody walks into our Mombasa Road showroom with a screenshot from a Japanese
auction site and the same question: "Why is your car more expensive than this?" It is a fair
question, and the honest answer is that the auction price is roughly half of the landed cost.

## What the auction price does not include

The hammer price at a Japanese auction is only the first line of the invoice. Before the car
can sit on a Kenyan road you also pay:

- Inland transport in Japan and port handling
- Ocean freight to Mombasa and marine insurance
- Import Duty at 25% of customs value
- Excise Duty, which scales with engine size and age
- VAT at 16%, calculated on top of duty and excise
- Import Declaration Fee and the Railway Development Levy
- Clearing agent fees, port storage and transport up to Nairobi
- NTSA registration, number plates and the logbook

By the time those land, a car that looked like KES 900,000 on the auction sheet is comfortably
past KES 1.8 million.

## The eight-year rule

Kenya does not allow the registration of vehicles more than eight years old from the year of
first registration. That rule moves every January. If you are importing privately, a shipping
delay of a few weeks can leave you with a car that cannot be registered at all — a risk that
sits entirely with you, not the exporter.

## Where a local purchase wins

When you buy from a yard that stands behind its stock, three things change:

1. **The inspection is already done.** Our used cars go through a 120-point check, and you
   read the report before you pay anything.
2. **The paperwork exists.** Logbook, import entry, duty receipts and NTSA transfer are in
   the file, not somewhere in a WhatsApp thread.
3. **There is somewhere to go back to.** A three-year powertrain warranty is only worth
   something if the seller still has a workshop in twelve months.

## How to compare fairly

Put both options on the same sheet. Write down the landed cost of the import including every
line above, add the value of your own time spent clearing it, and then subtract nothing for
risk — because risk is exactly what you are buying out of when you purchase locally.

If the import still comes out meaningfully cheaper for the exact same specification and
mileage, import it. If the gap is under about 10%, the local car is almost always the better
decision.
