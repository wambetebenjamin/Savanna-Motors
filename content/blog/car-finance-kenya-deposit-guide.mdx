Most buyers ask about the monthly repayment first. The deposit matters more, because it sets
the repayment, the approval odds and the total cost of credit all at once.

## The usual structure

Kenyan asset finance for a private vehicle generally looks like this:

- Deposit of 20% to 30% of the purchase price
- Tenure of 12 to 60 months — shorter for older used imports
- Interest of roughly 14% to 16% per annum on a reducing balance
- Facility fee of 1% to 2.5% and compulsory comprehensive insurance

Banks cap the tenure on older vehicles because they lend against the depreciation curve. For
a Japanese import that is already several years old, expect 36 to 48 months rather than 60.

## What the deposit actually changes

Take a KES 3,000,000 car at 15% over 48 months:

- **10% deposit** — finance KES 2,700,000, about KES 75,100 a month
- **20% deposit** — finance KES 2,400,000, about KES 66,800 a month
- **30% deposit** — finance KES 2,100,000, about KES 58,400 a month

The jump from 10% to 30% is roughly KES 16,700 a month, and over four years it saves you
around KES 300,000 in interest alone.

## Reducing balance versus flat rate

Always ask which one you are being quoted. A "12% flat" rate is not cheaper than "15%
reducing" — on a four-year loan it is considerably more expensive, because flat interest is
charged on the original amount for the whole term rather than on what you still owe.

## The costs people forget

Budget for these on day one, not in month three:

1. Comprehensive insurance, typically 3% to 4.5% of the car's value per year
2. Facility and valuation fees
3. NTSA transfer and logbook costs
4. The first service, tyres and anything the inspection report flagged

## A sensible rule of thumb

Keep the monthly repayment under 25% of your net monthly income, and keep the total of
repayment plus insurance plus fuel plus servicing under 35%. If a deal only works at the
edge of those numbers, choose a cheaper car rather than a longer tenure.
